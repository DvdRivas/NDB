Glioma

synonyms:
glial tumorglial cell tumorglial neoplasm

overview:
Summary
A glioma is a tumor of the central nervous system that arises from glial stem or progenitor cells. Glial cells are a type of cell widely present in the nervous system. Gliomas mostly occur in the brain and, rarely, in the spinal cord. They occur at various ages depending on the subtype. Gliomas can compress areas of the brain where they occur and cause various symptoms including headaches, nausea, vomiting, cognitive impairment, seizures, gait imbalance, language impairment (aphasia), numbness or weakness of one side of the body (hemiparesis), visual changes and personality changes. The treatment of gliomas often requires a combination of neurosurgical interventions, radiotherapy and chemotherapy.
Introduction
Classification of gliomas is based on the microscopic appearance of the tumor and the molecular characteristics such as gene changes (mutations) in the tumor. Tumors are often graded 1-4 based on severity, with 4 being the most aggressive. Information about specific tumor types is available from theNational Brain Tumor Society.

symptoms:
The symptoms associated with gliomas are similar among all types but can vary depending on the individual and the location of the tumor. Seizures (focal or generalized), language impairment (aphasia), weakness of part of the body (hemiparesis), sensory changes on part of the body and headaches are common. Other possible symptoms include gait disturbances, fatigue, dizziness, visual changes, vomiting and changes in urination. Psychological symptoms such as cognitive impairment, personality changes, depression, anxiety and memory impairment can also occur. Most of the symptoms are a consequence of the compressive effect of the tumor and fluid that surrounds it on the brain. Malignant gliomas (grade 3 and 4) are also associated with the development of blood clots in the deep veins, notably of the legs, (deep vein thrombosis) that can dislodge and migrate to occlude the arteries of the lungs (pulmonary embolism).
Gliomas can develop at any age. The average age at which they occur greatly varies depending on the subtype of glioma. Similarly, the survival rate greatly depends on the subtype of glioma.
In addition to being used for diagnosis and classification, the gene variants present in tumor cells are also used to predict disease course and survival (prognosis).
Over time, gliomas can increase in grade and, therefore, become more malignant (malignant progression). The rate of malignant progression depends on the subtype of glioma and on the genetic characteristics of affected cells. Higher grade tumors are typically associated with lower survival rates.
Cells in gliomas have an altered glucose metabolism and can develop their own blood vessel network which allows them to sustain the high energy requirements for cell division and growth. Inflammation and accumulation of fluid around the tumor are also features of gliomas. Over time, certain types of gliomas can grow and invade healthy brain tissue extensively.
The diagnosis and classification of brain tumors called gliomas have evolved over time, especially in the way the World Health Organization (WHO) categorizes them.
Previously, gliomas were primarily diagnosed by comparing tumor cells to normal brain cells. The main subtypes included:
Astrocytomas: These tumors start from cells called astrocytes that support the brain cells (neurons). These tumors can be either encapsulated, meaning they have clear boundaries from normal brain tissue, or infiltrative, which means they spread more into surrounding areas. Low-grade astrocytomas are more common in children, while high-grade ones are usually seen in young adults and older individuals.Oligodendrogliomas: These come from glial cells called oligodendrocytes that make a substance that protects nerve cells and helps with the flow of electrical signals in the brain and spinal cord. These tumors are generally less invasive than astrocytomas and are mostly found in adults.Ependymomas: These tumors originate from ependymal cells, which line the spaces in the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord. Ependymomas are more common in children.
Gliomas have been further categorized based on cell appearance, how often the cells divide (mitotic activity) and certain molecular markers. These categories helped determine if a tumor is low-grade (less aggressive), atypical or high-grade (more aggressive).
The latest WHO classification, released in 2021, uses more detailed molecular testing to better understand and categorize gliomas.
Molecular markers: The updated classification includes more molecular markers, which are specific characteristics found in the tumor’s DNA. These markers help in understanding how different types of gliomas behave and respond to treatments.DNA methylation profiling: This technique looks at specific patterns in the tumor’s DNA, providing information about genetic changes and how these tumors are likely to behave.
The new WHO grading system relies more on these molecular markers to determine the prognosis and the best treatment options for patients. In the new classification, gliomas, along with related tumor types, are categorized into several groups:
Adult-type diffuse gliomasPediatric-type diffuse low-grade gliomasPediatric-type diffuse high-grade gliomasCircumscribed astrocytic gliomasGlioneuronal and neuronal tumorsEpendymal tumors
Each of these categories can have different subtypes and are sometimes graded from 1 to 4, indicating their aggressiveness, with grade 1 being the least aggressive and grade 4 the most aggressive.
This classification helps doctors provide more precise diagnoses and determine the best treatment for individual patients based on the specific characteristics of their tumors.
